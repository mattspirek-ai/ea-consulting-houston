### P001 — Delegate your calendar before your inbox.

*short_text_insight · general*

Delegate your calendar before your inbox.

Most first-time delegators start with email. I'd start with the calendar, and here's why.

Your inbox needs weeks of context before anyone can run it: who matters, what tone you use, what you would never say. Get one reply wrong and trust resets to zero.

Your calendar runs on rules, and rules transfer on day one:

- No meetings before 9 or after 4.
- 25 and 50 minutes, never 30 and 60.
- Two hours of deep work daily, defended like a flight.
- Every meeting arrives with an agenda, or it arrives with a question.

An executive assistant can enforce rules like these in week one, and use that first win to earn the context that inbox work requires by week three.

Write your calendar rules down today, even if nobody runs them but you. That one page is the start of a delegation system.

### P006 — Track one week. Sort it into three lists.

*short_text_insight · general*

Track one week. Sort it into three lists.

Before you hire any support, run this exercise. It costs nothing and settles most delegation debates.

For five working days, keep a running note of everything you do longer than ten minutes. No judgment, just the log.

Friday afternoon, sort every entry into three lists:

1. Only me. Decisions, relationships, and judgment calls that genuinely require you.
2. Me, for now. Things someone could do with context: scheduling, travel, prep, follow-ups, expenses.
3. Anyone. Pure process that needs no context at all.

Two things happen almost every time. List one is smaller than you feared. And list two - the context work - is the bulk of your week.

List three you can automate or send anywhere. List two is exactly what a senior executive assistant exists for. List one is your actual job.

Most executives have never seen their week sorted this way. It changes the hiring conversation completely.

### P056 — Month-to-month means clients can fire us monthly.

*build_log_update · general*

Month-to-month means clients can fire us monthly.

Build log: the scariest term we chose, and why we chose it.

The safe version of this business runs on 12-month contracts. Revenue is predictable, churn is muffled, and a mediocre month costs you nothing because the client is locked in anyway. Most of the category works this way (source in comments).

We went month-to-month. Any client can walk at the end of any month.

What that costs us: planning certainty. A bench we have to size against revenue that can genuinely leave. Investors in this space would call it undisciplined.

What it buys: a forcing function. Every month, every engagement, someone re-decides whether we're worth it. Quality problems surface in weeks, not at renewal. And the sales conversation changes completely when "just try it for a month" is a real sentence.

Lock-in subsidizes complacency. We'd rather earn twelve renewals than sign one contract.

If this decision ever changes, you'll read about it here first, with the reasoning.
